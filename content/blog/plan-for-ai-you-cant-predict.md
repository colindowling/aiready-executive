You plan for AI you can't predict by committing to outcomes and principles, not specific tools, and planning in short cycles with regular review points. Make small, reversible bets, learn fast and keep options open. Waiting for certainty is its own decision, and usually the wrong one.

A marketing agency founder I talked with recently said what a lot of executives privately feel. He doesn't think people have any grasp on how to predict how AI will develop, and he expects how we do things to be completely different even by January 2027.

He's probably right. Which leaves leaders with a hard question: how do you plan when you can't predict?

## The tension: commitment versus flexibility

On one hand, boards want a plan. Teams want direction. Budgets need numbers. On the other hand, any detailed plan built on today's AI capabilities may be out of date in six months.

I land in the middle. Commit firmly to where you want to go. Stay flexible on how you get there.

## Anchor on outcomes, not tools

Tools will change. The outcomes you want probably won't. Start there:

- Which costs do you want to lower?
- Which customer experiences do you want to improve?
- Which decisions do you want to make faster or better?

Write those down. They're your strategy. Specific tools are just the current way of getting there.

## Plan in short cycles

Instead of a fixed three-year AI roadmap, try this:

1. **Set a direction** for the next year or two, based on outcomes.
2. **Plan initiatives** only a quarter or two ahead.
3. **Review every quarter.** What worked, what didn't, what's newly possible.
4. **Adjust.** Drop what isn't working. Double down on what is.

This is how you stay current without restarting every few months.

## Make reversible bets

Prefer decisions you can undo. Short contracts over long ones. Pilots before rollouts. Tools that work with your existing systems over ones that lock you in. When you're not sure what's coming, flexibility is worth a lot.

## Some things shouldn't wait

Not every decision should stay open. A few are needed no matter how AI evolves:

- **Data security and access rules.** Who can use what data, with which tools.
- **Ownership.** Who's responsible for AI efforts across the company.
- **Leadership literacy.** Your executive team needs hands-on familiarity, not just reports.

Waiting on those creates real risk.

## Learn faster than the change

The best defense against unpredictable change is a leadership team that learns quickly. Use the tools yourself. Have regular conversations about what's changing. Bring in outside perspective when you need it.

## What to tell your board

Be honest about the uncertainty. Boards don't expect you to predict AI. They expect you to have a way of deciding. Show them the outcomes you're pursuing, the few bets you're making this quarter, how you'll review them and what would make you change course. That answer is more credible than any confident long-range forecast.

## Signs your AI plan is too rigid, or missing

- You signed a multi-year AI contract before running a pilot.
- Your AI roadmap hasn't been revised since it was written.
- Leadership is "waiting to see how things settle" before deciding anything.
- Nobody on the executive team uses AI tools hands-on.

## What to do this week

Write down three business outcomes you want AI to help with over the next year. For each, name one small, reversible experiment you could start this quarter, and a date to review it. Bring that one page to your next leadership meeting. It's a plan that can bend, and it's more useful than a long roadmap that nobody updates.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for the technology to settle.** It won't, at least not soon.
- **Building the strategy around one vendor.** Tools change faster than outcomes.
- **Delegating AI understanding entirely.** Executives need hands-on familiarity to make good calls.
- **Skipping the quarterly review.** A roadmap nobody revisits is out of date by summer.

## Who should own this

A plan that adapts needs someone watching it. Name one executive as the owner, usually the CEO or a named AI sponsor on the executive team. Give them a clear goal, the authority to make the call and a regular slot to report back to the leadership team. Without a single owner, this becomes everyone's concern and no one's job, and it drifts until something goes wrong. With one, the questions above have someone accountable for answering them, and the board has one person to ask.
