Choose AI projects by starting with a specific business problem, not with the technology. List the processes that are slow, costly or error-prone, score each opportunity on value, feasibility and risk, fund only the best few, and define success before you start.

A marketing leader I spoke with recently said it simply: implementing AI just for the sake of doing so is wrong.

Plenty of companies are doing it anyway. Boards and investors ask about AI, so leaders feel pressure to show activity. The result is a pile of pilots that nobody can connect to results.

## Why "AI for AI's sake" happens

It's understandable. The pressure is real. Competitors announce AI initiatives. Vendors pitch AI everything. A board member asks what your AI strategy is.

The fastest response is to launch something. A chatbot, a pilot, a tool rollout. It shows motion. But motion isn't progress, and projects without a clear purpose tend to fade or fail.

## What it costs

- **Money.** Tools, consultants and time spent on things that don't matter.
- **Trust.** Each failed pilot makes the next one harder to sell internally.
- **Focus.** Teams get pulled from real work to support initiatives nobody needed.

## Start with the problem

Before any AI initiative, answer one question: what business problem does this solve?

Good answers are specific:

- "Our team spends 15 hours a week assembling the same report."
- "Customer response times are too slow on weekends."
- "Proposals take three days and should take one."

Bad answers are vague: "We need to be using AI." "Our competitors are doing it."

## Score before you fund

For each opportunity, ask:

1. **Value.** How much does solving this matter?
2. **Feasibility.** Do we have the data, systems and people to do it?
3. **Risk.** What could go wrong, and how bad would it be?

Fund the few with the best balance. Say no, or not yet, to the rest.

## Define success upfront

Every initiative should have a baseline, a target and a review date. If it doesn't hit the target after a fair trial, stop it or change it. Stopping a project that isn't working is a sign of discipline, not failure.

## Clean up what's already running

If you already have AI pilots underway, run them through the same test. For each one, ask what problem it solves, who owns it and what result it has produced so far. Keep the ones with clear answers. Pause or end the rest. A shorter list of AI work that matters is easier to fund, staff and explain.

## Tell the board the real story

When the board asks about AI, a short list of focused initiatives tied to business outcomes is a much stronger answer than a long list of pilots. It shows judgment, not just activity.

## Signs you're implementing AI for its own sake

- Nobody can say what problem your current AI pilot is meant to solve.
- Success is described as "adoption," not a business result.
- New AI tools arrive because a vendor pitched them, not because a team asked.
- Your AI update to the board is a list of pilots, not outcomes.

## What to do this week

Ask each department head for the single most costly, slow or error-prone process on their team. Score each answer on value, feasibility and risk. Choose one. Set a baseline and a target, then review it in 60 days.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the tool.** "We have licenses, now what?" leads to busywork.
- **Measuring adoption instead of outcomes.** Logins aren't results.
- **Keeping failing pilots alive.** Stopping one frees people for something better.
- **Picking the flashiest project first.** Pick the clearest problem first.
- **Hiding failures from the board.** A stopped pilot with lessons learned shows judgment.

## Who should own this

Prioritizing AI initiatives means saying no to some of them. Name one executive as the owner, usually the COO or the executive sponsoring AI. Give them a clear goal, the authority to make the call and a regular slot to report back to the leadership team. Without a single owner, this becomes everyone's concern and no one's job, and it drifts until something goes wrong. With one, the questions above have someone accountable for answering them, and the board has one person to ask.
