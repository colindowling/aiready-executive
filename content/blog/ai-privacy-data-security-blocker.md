To use AI safely with company data, classify your data, decide which approved tools can touch which kinds of data, and make the safe path easy with clear rules and training. Without that, people either avoid AI altogether or use it in ways nobody can see.

A founder I talked with recently, who works with companies adopting AI, said the number one concern he hears is privacy and data security.

That matches what most executives report. It's also a concern that, left unanswered, quietly stalls adoption.

## Two bad outcomes

When there are no clear rules, companies tend to end up in one of two places.

**People avoid AI.** They're worried about doing something wrong, so they don't use it. The company falls behind.

**People use it anyway.** They paste company or customer data into personal accounts and unapproved tools. Leaders don't know it's happening. The risk is real and invisible.

Both are worse than having clear rules.

## Start by classifying data

Not all data carries the same risk. Sort it into a few simple categories:

- **Public.** Already published. Low risk.
- **Internal.** Everyday business information. Moderate risk.
- **Confidential.** Financials, strategy, contracts. Higher risk.
- **Regulated or personal.** Customer data, health data, employee records. Highest risk.

Then decide which tools can be used with each category.

## Approve the right tools

Business versions of major AI tools typically offer controls that consumer versions don't, like limits on data retention and use for training. Review the options, approve a small set and make them easy to access.

If the approved tools are hard to get, people will use whatever's easiest.

## Write a short, practical policy

A good policy fits on a page:

1. Which tools are approved.
2. What data can go into each.
3. What's never allowed.
4. Who to ask when unsure.
5. How it's monitored.

Short policies get read. Long ones get ignored.

## Train and review

Walk teams through the policy with real examples. Revisit it every few months, because tools and risks change quickly. Look for signs of unapproved use and respond by offering a safe alternative, not just a warning.

## Questions to ask your team this week

Ask three simple questions. Which AI tools are people using right now, including personal accounts? What kinds of data are going into them? Do people know what's allowed? The answers are often surprising, and they're rarely a sign of bad intent. People are usually just trying to get their work done faster. They give you a realistic starting point for a policy, instead of one written for how you assume people work.

## Make safe the easy option

The goal isn't to lock AI down. It's to make safe use simple, so teams can move fast inside clear guardrails.

## Signs data concerns are stalling you

- Teams say "we're waiting for legal" about AI, with no date attached.
- There's no list of approved AI tools anywhere.
- Some teams use AI heavily and others not at all, with no clear reason why.
- Nobody can say what data has already gone into which tools.

## A worked example

Say a sales team wants to use AI to summarize call notes. Without a policy, one rep pastes customer details into a personal account and another refuses to touch AI at all. With a one-page policy, the answer is clear: call notes count as internal data, the approved business tool may be used, and customer financial details are excluded. The safe path is now also the easy one.

## What to do this week

Ask each department head which AI tools their team uses and what data goes in. Draft a one-page policy from the answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a long policy.** Nobody reads twenty pages.
- **Banning everything.** People route around bans with personal accounts.
- **Approving tools slowly.** If approval takes months, shadow AI fills the gap.
- **Treating it as an IT-only issue.** Data rules shape how every team works, so leadership has to own them.

## Who should own this

AI data rules cut across IT, legal and every business team. Name one executive as the owner, usually the CIO, CTO or COO. Give them a clear goal, the authority to make the call and a regular slot to report back to the leadership team. Without a single owner, this becomes everyone's concern and no one's job, and it drifts until something goes wrong. With one, the questions above have someone accountable for answering them, and the board has one person to ask.
