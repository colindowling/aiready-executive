AI probably won't replace salespeople entirely, but it will likely mean fewer of them. A lot of sales work exists because buying is full of friction, and AI is removing some of it. Executives should plan for smaller teams focused on complex, high-trust deals, and redesign roles now rather than react later.

Two people I spoke with recently, both with long careers in go-to-market, came at this from different angles and landed close together.

One pointed out that roles like sales engineering exist because there's friction in the process of adopting technology. As AI removes that friction, those roles shrink. He also called the idea that buyers will always want a human a crock, because if you offered them a service that was 20 percent cheaper without one, plenty would take it.

The other's hot take was simpler: there are going to be a lot fewer sellers.

## The friction argument

Think about what a lot of sales work actually is. Answering standard questions. Explaining how the product works. Running the same demo. Scheduling. Following up. Pulling together information for the buyer's internal approval.

Much of that exists because buyers couldn't easily get answers on their own. AI is changing that. Buyers have never been more prepared, and they increasingly arrive having already asked an AI tool most of their questions.

When the friction goes, so does some of the work.

## The "humans are premium" assumption

Many executives assume buyers will always value a human in the sale. Some will, especially for complex or risky purchases.

But be honest about the rest. If a buyer can get the same result faster or cheaper without a salesperson, many will choose that. Building your plan on the assumption that a human sale is always preferred is risky.

## Where humans still win

I'm a big believer that people still buy from people they trust, especially when the stakes are high. Humans add the most value where:

- The deal is complex, with many stakeholders.
- The risk is high and trust matters.
- The problem isn't well defined yet.
- Negotiation and judgment shape the outcome.

That's where your best sellers should spend their time.

## What to plan for now

Don't wait for the market to decide for you. Start planning:

1. **Map the work.** Where do sellers spend their hours today?
2. **Sort it.** What can AI handle, what needs a person, what's in between?
3. **Redesign roles.** Fewer, more senior roles focused on high-value deals.
4. **Invest in skills.** Discovery, business sense, relationship building.
5. **Update comp and hiring.** Incentives built for volume won't fit.

Move gradually and measure. Cutting first and hoping AI fills the gap rarely goes well.

## Don't forget the people

How you handle this matters to your culture. Be open with your sales team about what's changing and why. Give people a path to the roles that will grow, with training to get there. Teams that understand the plan tend to help shape it. Teams that hear rumors tend to leave before you're ready.

## Signs your sales model is exposed

- Much of your sellers' time goes to answering the same standard questions.
- Buyers arrive at first calls already knowing your pricing and features.
- Smaller deals still go through the full sales process.
- Comp plans reward activity volume more than deal outcomes.

## What to do this week

Ask your sales leader for a rough breakdown of how sellers spend a typical week: research, admin, standard questions, demos, real conversations with decision-makers. Mark which parts AI could handle now. That list, not a headcount target, is the right starting point for planning.

## Common mistakes

- **Cutting headcount before redesigning roles.** You lose the people who know your customers.
- **Assuming every buyer wants a human.** Many will take faster or cheaper.
- **Assuming no buyer wants a human.** Complex, high-stakes deals still need people.
- **Leaving comp plans unchanged.** Old incentives fight new roles.

## Who should own this

Redesigning the sales team affects revenue, people and budgets. Name one executive as the owner, usually the CRO working closely with the CFO and head of people. Give them a clear goal, the authority to make the call and a regular slot to report back to the leadership team. Without a single owner, this becomes everyone's concern and no one's job, and it drifts until something goes wrong. With one, the questions above have someone accountable for answering them, and the board has one person to ask.
