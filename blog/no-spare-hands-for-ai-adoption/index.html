No Spare Hands: How to Find Capacity for AI Adoption When the Day Job Still Exists

Teams want to adopt AI but have no time beyond their day jobs. Here's how executives can create real capacity for AI adoption without burning people out.

November 1, 2026 · Colin Dowling

Key takeaways

  • Having the hands to actually adopt AI is one of the biggest challenges, because day jobs still exist.
  • Adoption fails when it's added on top of full workloads.
  • Executives must make room: stop something, fund time, name an owner.
  • Start with one workflow and finish it before starting the next.

To find capacity for AI adoption, executives have to make room on purpose: stop or pause something, give specific people dedicated hours, name an owner, and start with one workflow you finish before you begin the next. Adoption added on top of a full workload almost always stalls.

A go-to-market consultant I talked with recently named the problem clearly. Figuring out how to adopt AI and having the hands to actually do it is one of the biggest challenges teams have right now, because those day jobs still exist.

That's the part executives tend to underestimate.

Why "just use AI" doesn't work

The message from the top is often some version of: we need to be using AI. Go figure it out.

So people try, in the gaps between meetings, on top of their targets and deadlines. They sign up for tools, try a few prompts, maybe build something small. Then the quarter gets busy and it all stops.

Nobody failed. There just wasn't any room.

Adoption is real work

Adopting AI well isn't a matter of buying a tool. It means:

  • Understanding the current workflow in detail.
  • Deciding what to change and testing it.
  • Training people and updating processes.
  • Maintaining it as tools change.

That's a project. Projects need time and owners.

How to make room

Stop something

The cleanest way to create capacity is to stop doing something lower-value. Every team has reports nobody reads or meetings that could end. Cut one, and give that time to AI work.

Fund the time

Give specific people a set number of hours each week for AI adoption, protected from other work. A few people with real time will do more than a whole team with none.

Name an owner

Each initiative needs one person responsible for it. Not a committee. One name.

Get outside help for the heavy lifting

Setup and maintenance can be handed to advisors or managed services, so your team focuses on deciding what to change instead of building everything themselves.

Start with one workflow

Pick one process that's repetitive, time-consuming and well understood. Improve it with AI. Measure the time saved. Then reinvest part of that time into the next workflow.

Finishing one thing well beats starting ten. It builds confidence, and it creates the capacity for what comes next.

What this looks like in practice

Picture a ten-person team asked to adopt AI with no change in workload. Six months later, a few enthusiasts have tried things and nothing has stuck. Now picture the same team with one person given a day a week, one workflow to fix and a review date. The second team usually has something working within a quarter.

Signs capacity is the real blocker

  • Your company has bought AI licenses that few people use.
  • AI comes up in every leadership meeting, but nobody's calendar has time set aside for it.
  • The same few enthusiasts do all the experimenting, on nights and weekends.
  • Pilots start strong and fade once the quarter gets busy.

What to do this week

Pick one workflow and one person. Give that person four hours a week for the next six weeks, protected on the calendar, to improve that workflow with AI. Decide what they'll stop doing to make room. Set a review at the end of the six weeks with a simple question: how much time did it save, and is it worth doing the next one? That's a real start, and it fits inside a quarter.

Common mistakes

  • Asking everyone to experiment a little. A little time from everyone adds up to nothing finished.
  • Starting five pilots at once. Finish one first.
  • Not saying what stops. If nothing comes off the plate, adoption is the thing that slips.
  • Treating enthusiasts' spare time as a plan. It isn't sustainable.

Who should own this

Capacity decisions mean deciding what stops. Name one executive as the owner, usually the COO or the executive sponsoring AI adoption. Give them a clear goal, the authority to make the call and a regular slot to report back to the leadership team. Without a single owner, this becomes everyone's concern and no one's job, and it drifts until something goes wrong. With one, the questions above have someone accountable for answering them, and the board has one person to ask.

Questions

Why is AI adoption so slow in many companies?

Often because nobody has time. Teams are asked to learn new tools and redesign workflows on top of full workloads. Without dedicated time, clear ownership and leadership support, AI adoption becomes an afterthought. Lack of capacity is frequently a bigger barrier than cost or technology.

How can executives create time for AI adoption?

Stop or pause lower-value work, dedicate a few hours a week for specific people to work on AI, or bring in outside help for the setup. Name an owner for each initiative. Treat adoption as a real project with a goal and deadline, not a side task people fit in.

Should we hire someone to lead AI adoption?

It can help, but it isn't always necessary. Many companies start with an internal owner given dedicated time, supported by outside advisors or managed services for setup and maintenance. What matters most is that someone has clear responsibility and real capacity, not just an extra title.

What's the best first step in AI adoption for a busy team?

Pick one workflow that's repetitive, time-consuming and well understood. Automate or improve it with AI, measure the time saved and reinvest some of that time in the next workflow. Finishing one thing well builds momentum and frees capacity for what comes next.
