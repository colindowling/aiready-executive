Human Judgment Is Still the Job When AI Does the Busywork

AI can draft, sort and summarize. Leaders still set the goal, the constraints and the feedback. Here's how executives keep judgment where it belongs.

November 6, 2026 · Colin Dowling

Key takeaways

  • AI can take the busywork. Judgment stays with people.
  • Your job is goals, constraints and continuous feedback.
  • Without those three, AI just produces more noise faster.
  • Boards care who decides. Name that person before you scale the tools.

AI is getting good at the parts of work nobody loved doing. Drafting the first pass. Sorting the inbox. Summarizing the binder. That doesn't mean the hard part went away. The hard part was always deciding what matters, what the limits are and how to steer when the first answer is wrong.

An engineering leader I spoke with on my podcast put it plainly: human judgment is still needed. What are you trying to accomplish? What constraints do you set for the AI, or for the organization around it? And how do you give feedback continuously so the work becomes what it needs to be?

That triad is the job. The tools change. The job doesn't.

Busywork is not the same as the decision

In a plant, a warehouse or a project office, AI can pull exceptions from overnight logs, draft a change-order summary or flag clauses in a bid package. Useful. None of that answers whether you should stop the line, re-sequence the truck or take the job.

I land here: let AI do the prep. Keep a named person on the call that carries risk, money or reputation.

The tension: more output vs. more ownership

On the one hand, every vendor promises that AI will "just handle it." On the other, your board, your customers and your insurers still want a human they can look in the eye when something goes sideways.

I kind of land in the middle. Automate the busywork hard. Keep judgment on three things: the goal, the constraints and the feedback loop. Everything else is optional.

What judgment looks like in practice

  • Goals. "Reduce warehouse exception resolution time this week" beats "use AI more."
  • Constraints. Which data the tool may touch, which systems it may write to, what it may never decide alone.
  • Feedback. A daily or weekly review where people correct the AI and those corrections stick.

An example

Say a logistics firm gets two hundred overnight exception emails. AI sorts them into late trucks, damaged freight and paperwork gaps, then drafts a short action list for the ops lead. The lead still decides which customers get a call before 8 a.m., which loads get reassigned and which claims go to legal. The AI shrank the morning from ninety minutes to fifteen. The judgment stayed with the person who owns the customer.

Signs you've handed the job to the tool

  • Nobody can say what "good" looked like for this week's AI work.
  • Constraints live in a Slack thread nobody can find.
  • Feedback never gets written back into the system.
  • Staff say "the AI decided" when a customer asks who approved it.

What to do this week

Pick one workflow where AI already drafts or sorts. Write three lines: the goal for that workflow, the hard constraints and who reviews the output before it leaves the building. Share those three lines with the team that uses the tool.

Common mistakes

  • Treating volume as progress. More drafts aren't a win if nobody set the target.
  • Vague constraints. "Be careful with data" is not a rule. Name systems, fields and decisions.
  • One-and-done prompts. Judgment is a loop. Schedule the feedback.
  • No named owner. If the board asks who decided, you need a name, not a model.

Who should own this

The function leader owns the goals and constraints for their area. The CEO owns the company rule: AI prepares, people decide, and here's who. That answer covers most of the governance questions a board will ask about AI.

Keep the judgment. Shrink the busywork.

Questions

Does AI replace executive judgment?

No. It can draft, sort and summarize. Leaders still set goals, constraints and the feedback that shapes the work.

What should stay human when we adopt AI?

Anything that carries customer, safety, financial or reputation risk. Also the definition of success for each workflow.

How do I explain this to a board?

Give one rule: AI does the busywork, people keep the judgment, and name who decides in each area. Then show the feedback loop.
